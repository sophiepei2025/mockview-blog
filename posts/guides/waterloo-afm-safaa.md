## Waterloo SAFAA Overview

The SAFAA is mandatory for all applicants to Waterloo's School of Accounting and Finance.

<div class="institution-callout">
  <strong>Worth knowing:</strong> After you apply through OUAC, you'll receive a follow-up email with links to complete the SAFAA components — video interview, trait assessment, and optionally the AIF. These have their own separate deadline, later than the OUAC deadline itself, and both the interview and trait assessment are completed on third-party platforms. Deadlines shift slightly year to year, so always confirm the current dates directly on <a href="https://uwaterloo.ca/future-students/programs/accounting-and-financial-management">Waterloo's SAF admissions page</a> rather than a date from a past cycle.
</div>

The SAFAA has two parts:

1. **Interview** — a mix of video and written questions, including a consent statement
2. **Trait Assessment** — a written questionnaire where you rate statements on an agree/disagree scale

## How Waterloo SAF Evaluates Applicants

Admissions considers three main factors: the interview, the trait assessment, and academic performance. Since grades only tell part of the story, the interview and trait assessment carry real weight — they're where you show communication skills, self-awareness, and fit for the program in ways a transcript can't.

Waterloo SAF is generally looking for applicants who:

- Communicate clearly, concisely, and professionally
- Show genuine passion and follow-through, not just participation
- Bring a range of extracurricular, volunteer, or work experience
- Can reflect on their own experiences and show real growth from them
- Collaborate well and stay open to different ways of thinking

## Part 1: Video Interview

The interview typically includes a short set of practice questions first (so you can get comfortable with the format before anything counts), followed by a mix of video and written questions with a short prep window and a time limit per response. The whole thing usually takes around 20–30 minutes.

<div class="institution-callout">
  <strong>Tip:</strong> The practice questions are often the same as the real ones — treat them as a genuine rehearsal, not a throwaway warm-up. You typically get multiple attempts on the practice round, so use it to actually refine your answer before the version that counts.
</div>

You'll also need a photo ID ready to upload before you begin, so have it scanned and accessible ahead of time rather than scrambling for it mid-interview.

### Sample Question Type: Teamwork & Diversity

A typical prompt in this category asks you to describe a time you worked in a team with diverse backgrounds and perspectives, hit a challenge or setback, and worked through it together — and how that experience shapes how you'll collaborate going forward.

The point of a question like this isn't really the project itself — it's *how* you worked with the team. Waterloo SAF wants to see that you can genuinely learn from people who think differently than you do, not just tolerate them. Diverse teams are well-documented to outperform homogeneous ones precisely because different perspectives surface blind spots a like-minded group would miss entirely.

A strong answer usually does a few things: gives just enough context to understand the situation, is honest about what the actual friction or setback was (not a sanitized non-problem), shows a specific moment where a teammate's different perspective changed the outcome, and closes with something concrete about how that's changed how you approach group work since — not just "it taught me the importance of teamwork."

### Sample Question Type: ESG & Corporate Responsibility

Another common prompt asks about your familiarity with Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) metrics, and what role you think the business community should play in addressing issues like climate change and equity.

**ESG, briefly:** these are non-financial measures of how sustainably and responsibly a company operates, generally split into three areas — environmental (emissions, waste, resource use), social (employee health and safety, product safety), and governance (board diversity, ethical conduct and decision-making). Companies, investors, and customers all use these metrics differently, but the common thread is assessing whether a business is actually being responsible, not just claiming to be.

For this kind of question, avoid the generic answer ("companies should reduce emissions") — that shows you know the term but not that you've thought about it. Split your answer into the relevant areas (environmental, social, and governance where applicable), give one or two specific and slightly less obvious examples of what businesses can actually do, and — importantly — connect it to something personal. Even though the question sounds abstract, the strongest answers reveal something about the applicant's own values and how they arrived at them, not just a textbook definition.

<div class="institution-callout">
  <strong>A note on originality:</strong> Example answers exist to show you the shape of a strong response, never to be copied. Plagiarized responses are usually detectable and can result in immediate disqualification — use examples as a reference point, not a script.
</div>
