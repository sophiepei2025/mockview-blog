## 1. UBC Science (BSc): Program Overview and the 2026/2027 Application Process

### 1.1 How the program works
The University of British Columbia's Bachelor of Science (B.Sc.) is a **four-year undergraduate program**. Students admitted from high school spend first year in a **general science foundation year (General Science Year 1)** and do not choose a specific major or specialization right away.

After completing first year and meeting the **second-year class standing requirements**, students apply to a specialization such as Biology, Biochemistry, Neuroscience, Computer Science or Data Science.

* **A warning about choosing a major (the pre-med myth):**
  When planning first-year courses and your future specialization, **don't crowd into a popular subject like Biology just because it "sounds pre-med."** Canadian medical schools give no official preference to any undergraduate major. They assess GPA, MCAT and your overall record. Forcing yourself into a subject you're not strong in or don't enjoy can hurt your GPA and cost you your advantage. Build on your real strengths and interests, and choose the specialization where you can keep your GPA highest and show academic excellence.

### 1.2 Two-track assessment: holistic review in an era of grade inflation
UBC uses **holistic review**, with academics and the Personal Profile assessed side by side:

1. **Academic performance:** Grades 11 and 12 academic courses, especially the science prerequisites (pre-calculus/calculus, chemistry, biology, physics and English).
2. **Personal Profile:** With **grade inflation** widespread in Canada and elsewhere, the Personal Profile now carries more weight than ever. Public data show that **over 70% of high school students make the honour roll**, and applicants to top science programs commonly hold **90% to 97%+ averages**. Top grades are now just a status check, and **the Personal Profile has become the primary differentiator** that decides who stands out from tens of thousands of similar high-achieving applicants.

### 1.3 Timing: starting in October vs. scrambling in December or January
How early you start and how deeply you plan set the ceiling on your application:

```
[Start in October]  (recommended: a 6–8 week window)
  └─ Time to dig into real motivation ──> brief your references well ──> keep your own voice ──> mindset: doing it right

[Scramble in Dec/Jan]  (high risk: settling)
  └─ Late-night rewrites before the deadline ──> last-minute reference requests ──> generic clichés ──> mindset: just getting it done
```

* **The cost of a December/January rush:** Your mindset slides from "doing it right" to "just getting it done." Last-minute work leaves no room for real self-reflection, references who are asked too late can't verify much, and a profile full of clichés is quickly passed over.
* **The advantage of starting in October:** Six to eight weeks lets you do real **subterranean self-exploration**, build a solid **experience bank**, iterate on your reflection several times, and pass the replace-the-name test so your writing has a voice no one else could fake.

### 1.4 Key milestones
* **October (structure and inventory):**
  * Start planning and list every academic and extracurricular experience from the last three years in an experience bank.
  * Use the **T-model** to pick 1–2 anchor activities and 2–3 supporting activities.
  * Choose and contact your two references (at least one must be a school official) and discuss what your Personal Profile will cover.
* **November (first draft and STAR restructuring):**
  * Draft the Personal Profile, keeping long answers to a strict structure (Situation/Task 15%, Action 55%, Reflection 30%).
  * Control the character count of every activity description and make sure each has strong verbs and impact metrics.
  * Run your first **replace-the-name test** and cut every cliché.
* **Early December (final polish with a single source of truth):**
  * Polish with one trusted reviewer; asking too many people dilutes your voice.
  * Check activity summaries stay within the 500-character limit.
  * Finish proofreading before the recommended deadline (the priority window is usually December 1; the final deadline is January 15).
* **Late December / early January (submit and confirm references):**
  * Submit through UBC's system, avoiding the peak-traffic rush.
  * Confirm both references have verified your activities through UBC's official system.

## 2. Submitting the Personal Profile and Choosing Activities (Step-by-Step)

### Process 1: choosing activities with the T-model

* Step 1: List every experience from the past three years (research, competitions, clubs, volunteering, part-time work, sports and arts, family or community responsibilities)
  * `-->` Step 2: Filter with the **T-model** (the system allows at most five activity entries)
    * **Depth (1–2 anchor activities):** the activities you've invested in longest (1–2+ years), with clear **ownership** and **measurable impact**
    * **Breadth (2–3 supporting activities):** activities that show transferable soft skills such as collaboration across disciplines, resilience or community responsibility
  * `-->` Step 3: Order the entries so your strongest anchor activity is in the first slot
  * `-->` Step 4: Tighten each summary (strictly within the **500-character limit**) around a strong action verb and concrete, quantified results

### Process 2: completing and submitting the Personal Profile

* Step 1: Answer the background prompts (who you are, how friends, family or your community would describe you, what matters most to you and why)
  * `-->` Step 2: Fill in activities and achievements (up to five, each with name, role, organization, duration and an impact summary **under 500 characters**)
  * `-->` Step 3: Choose 1–2 core experiences for the long-answer questions (use STAR, and spend most of your words on specific action and the change in your thinking)
  * `-->` Step 4: Add academic context if needed (grade dips, dropped courses or circumstances outside your control; optional)
  * `-->` Step 5: Enter your two references
    * **Rule 1:** at least one must be a school official (a counsellor, vice-principal or core-subject teacher)
    * **Rule 2:** at least one must be able to confirm specific details of a core activity in your profile
  * `-->` Step 6: Do a final replace-the-name test and a single-source review, submit in UBC's system, and follow up on reference confirmations

## 3. The Four Personal Profile Scoring Criteria

UBC's trained readers score the Personal Profile on four core criteria (on a 1–5 scale). Readers go through thousands of profiles each year and have strong **pattern recognition**: any abstract claim without supporting fact is instantly flagged as generic.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                     UBC Personal Profile: four scoring criteria                    |
+-------------------+-------------------+-------------------+-----------------------+
|  Engagement       |  Leadership       |  Substance        |  Voice                |
+-------------------+-------------------+-------------------+-----------------------+
```

### 1. Engagement
* **What readers look for:** how long you've been involved (**longevity**) and the real **responsibility** you carried.
* **How pattern recognition works:** readers can tell at a glance whether a résumé was padded in Grade 12 or reflects long-term commitment. A high Engagement score doesn't require international awards. Caring for younger siblings over years, training new staff at a restaurant job, or building a long-running shared study resource for classmates all score well if they show sustained responsibility and effort.

### 2. Leadership
* **Core rule:** **leadership does not require a title.**
* **How pattern recognition works:** readers dislike applicants who hold titles like president or captain but produced nothing. They look at how you **took initiative**, **found resources** and **changed the situation**. Spotting a real problem in a team or community and stepping up to fix it (for example, redesigning a partnership model to save a club after it lost its sponsor) is real leadership.

### 3. Substance: closing the translation gap
* **What it means:** answers must be specific and grounded in facts and numbers. Low-substance lines like "volunteering taught me to help others" don't score.
* **The translation gap:**
  * **Listing (scores 1–2):** describing what happened in order or listing duties ("I was responsible for guiding students and managing budget."). This is where readers mentally check out.
  * **Translating (scores 5):** going beyond the event to explain the change in your thinking, the logic behind it and its measurable impact.

$$\text{Event (Listing)} + \text{Shift in thinking and concrete action (Translating)} = \text{Full-marks Substance}$$

| | Listing (low score) | Translating (high score) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **What it looks like** | Activity names and duties plus abstract labels (like *"I developed strong communication skills"*). | Goes straight to the moment of friction, explains the action and shows a **cognitive shift**. |
| **Reader's reaction** | Pattern-matched as "another similar applicant" and skimmed. | Sees real, concrete **evidence** and remembers it. |

### 4. Voice: the authenticity test
* **What it means:** the writing must sound like **you**, with your own perspective, real feeling and reflection, not an over-edited, templated or AI-polished essay with its personality removed (**voice flattening**).
* **The replace-the-name test:**
  * **How to run it:** replace your name with a classmate's and reread.
  * **How to judge it:** if the piece could belong to anyone, its Voice score is very low. Only writing marked by your own setbacks, decisions and deeper motivation (your **subterranean why**) earns a full Voice score.

## 5. UBC Personal Profile Dos & Don'ts

### 5.1 Dos and don'ts at a glance

| Dos | Don'ts |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Follow the STAR ratio:** put most of your words into **Action (55%)** and **Reflection (30%)**; keep Situation/Task to 15%. | **Cliché openings:** never start with *"I have always been passionate about science..."* or *"Since I was young..."*. |
| **Open with a concrete hook:** start with a moment of failure, an unexpected detail or a specific situation to break the reader's pattern recognition. | **Buzzwords and labels:** avoid *"strong communication skills,"* *"hard worker"* and *"natural leader."* |
| **Use strong verbs and numbers:** *Led, Initiated, Engineered, Rebuilt* plus hard data to show impact. | **Quantity over quality:** five ordinary activities are worth less than one or two deep T-model anchors. |
| **Keep a single source of truth:** one trusted advisor keeps your strategy consistent and your voice distinct. | **Too many reviewers:** asking seven different people produces a compromise essay with no voice. |
| **Keep your real high-school voice:** leave in honest rough edges and signs of growth so you pass the replace-the-name test. | **Over-relying on AI:** heavy AI polishing makes sentences symmetrical and hollow and flattens your voice. |

### 5.2 The strategies in detail

#### 1. The STAR ratio
In the long-answer questions, don't spread your words evenly. Readers don't care about a long backstory; they care about what you did and how deeply you thought about it:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                               STAR word allocation                                 |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
| Situation / Task (~15%)            | Set the scene and goal in no more than two   |
|                                    | sentences                                    |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
| Action (~55%)                      | The specific steps "I" took, the resistance  |
|                                    | I met and my strategy                        |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
| Result / Reflection (~30%)         | Measurable results + the shift in thinking   |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
```

* **What full-marks reflection looks like:** not "I learned teamwork is important," but a real **cognitive shift**.
  * *Weak reflection:* "I learned that a leader needs to communicate more with the team."
  * *Strong reflection:* "I realized I had misunderstood leadership as giving orders. Real leadership is building a structure, under pressure, where even the quietest teammate feels safe to speak and contribute."

#### 2. The opening hook: breaking pattern recognition
After thousands of profiles, *"I have always loved science"* or *"Science is my passion"* makes readers check out.

* **How to build a real hook:**
  * **Start with a counter-intuitive failure:** "When I found the enzyme assay tube precipitating again on my fourth controlled trial, throwing the optical density data off…"
  * **Start with a concrete conflict:** "With 21 days until the science fair, the sponsor covering 45% of our budget suddenly pulled out…"
  * A vivid moment pulls the reader into the next line.

#### 3. Strong verbs and numbers
* **Verbs to swap in:**
  * Replace *Provided / Helped* $\rightarrow$ with **Engineered**, **Initiated**, **Rebuilt**, **Mobilized**, **Negotiated**.
* **Numbers beat vagueness:** turn "I raised a lot of money and helped many people" into "mobilized 25 volunteers, partnered with two organizations, raised $20,000 and reached 100+ seniors." Data is the strongest cure for empty writing.

#### 4. A warning about the single source of truth
* **The admission-killer pattern:** anxious applicants ask seven people — parents, teachers, Reddit, classmates, relatives. Parents want more emotion, teachers want it academic, the forum wants a new structure, friends want humour. After seven rounds of edits the essay becomes a contradictory, edgeless "design by committee" with no voice.
* **The fix:** choose **one** trusted reviewer with real experience of Canadian university admissions who understands your story, and follow one clear strategy throughout.

#### 5. Buzzwords and AI voice flattening
* **Drop the buzzwords:** *"passionate,"* *"hard worker,"* *"leader"* and *"good communicator"* have been used by hundreds of thousands of applicants. Without a specific event behind them, they don't add points; they mark you as lacking Substance.
* **Guard against AI voice flattening:** over-relying on AI is one of the reasons rejections have risen in recent years. AI text tends toward symmetrical structures, ornate vocabulary and hollow sentiment, flattening your voice. Readers can spot this "assembly-line" writing in seconds. Keep your own thinking, the real emotion in your setbacks and the maturity of a high school student; that's what passes the replace-the-name test and earns an offer.
