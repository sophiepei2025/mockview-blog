## 1. Before You Start (About 5 Minutes)

Before your first full mock, get these things in place so your first score is worth using as a reference:

* **Pick one target school.** Practise one at a time rather than opening three at once. Live now: UofT Engineering, McMaster Engineering and Queen's Commerce.
* **Read that school's guide first.** Learn its question types, timing and scoring dimensions: [UofT Engineering](/en/blog/uoft-engineering-online-profile-guide-2026-2027/), [McMaster Engineering](/en/blog/mcmaster-engineering-supplementary-application-guide-2026-2027/) or [Queen's Commerce](/en/blog/queens-commerce-smith-supplementary-application-guide-2026-2027/).
* **Set up your space.** A quiet room, front lighting, the camera at eye level and a stable connection, with no notes or script next to the screen.
* **Prepare 5–8 real stories.** Use the story bank method in the [Kira Talent Interview Prep Guide](/en/blog/kira-talent-practice-guide-2026/), covering leadership, conflict, failure, adaptability and similar scenarios.

---

## 2. Your First Full Mock: Treat It as Real

The point of the first run is a **true baseline**, so keep to real conditions as closely as you can:

1. **No script and no pausing.** If you freeze, pause for a second, collect your thoughts and finish. That's exactly what you're practising.
2. **Do the whole flow.** Follow the target school's format from start to finish (a full run takes about 18 minutes) rather than picking a single question.
3. **Don't rush to fix things yet.** Read the report, write down the overall band and each dimension's score, and treat that as your starting point.

---

## 3. How to Read the Report: Three Steps

When you get your report, read it in this order:

1. **Start with the overall band and each dimension's score.** Find the one or two lowest dimensions.
2. **Then look at the lines that quote your own words.** The report points to the specific sentence that caused a problem, such as being too general, missing numbers, or a reflection that stops at "I learned teamwork matters."
3. **Finally, read the English fixes.** Each suggestion comes with a sentence you can say aloud. Treat it as a model for how to say it better.

### Common causes of low scores and how to fix them

| Dimension | Common cause of a low score | How to fix it next time |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Program fit** | The answer could apply to any school and doesn't connect to this program. | Add a detail specific to the program (a course, a teaching approach, a research area) and explain how it matches your experience. |
| **Narrative & structure** | A chronological account, with the situation using up half your time and squeezing out action and reflection. | Reallocate time with STAR+T: situation under 15%, about half for action, and 15%–20% for reflection. |
| **Specificity** | Abstract claims ("I'm a good leader") with no numbers or concrete actions. | Add a measurable result and a specific action; say what you did instead of what you are. |
| **Reflection & delivery** | A cliché reflection; speaking too fast, too many fillers, not looking at the lens. | Write reflection as "past approach → key realisation → what I do now," and use a deliberate one-second pause instead of "um." |

## 4. Rewrite and Practise Again: Fix Only the Weakest Dimension

After you read the report, don't try to fix everything at once. You won't be able to tell what made a difference.

* **Focus on one weak dimension per round.** For example, work on specificity this round and reflection the next.
* **Turn the English fixes into your own words.** They're models, not scripts to memorise. Memorising flattens your voice and costs points. Keep the structure and say it with your own experience and phrasing.
* **Practise several different questions of the same type.** Repeating a single question trains you to memorise that question, not to answer that kind of question.
* **Always keep the timer on.** Don't skip the countdown because it's stressful. A sense of time is a skill you need to practise.

---

## 5. A 7-Day Practice Plan

Here is a rhythm you can follow directly, about 30–45 minutes a day including reading the report and rewriting:

| Day | Focus | What to do |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Day 1** | Set a baseline | Do one full mock and record the overall band and each dimension. Don't change anything yet. |
| **Day 2** | Structure | Rework your stories for "narrative & structure," allocate time with STAR+T and practise 2–3 questions. |
| **Day 3** | Specificity | Add numbers and concrete actions to each story and practise 2–3 more questions. |
| **Day 4** | Reflection | Rewrite each story's ending as "past approach → realisation → what I do now" and practise again. |
| **Day 5** | Camera and pacing | Concentrate on looking at the lens, controlling your pace and using pauses in place of fillers. |
| **Day 6** | Mixed questions | Mix question types without choosing, to simulate real uncertainty. |
| **Day 7** | Full review | Do one more full mock and compare it with your Day 1 baseline to see which dimensions improved and which still need work. |

After seven days, if a dimension is still low, run another targeted round, or ask an advisor for a one-on-one diagnosis of that dimension.

---

## 6. Common Mistakes

* **Treating the AI score as an admissions prediction.** It's a practice reference for finding problems, not a forecast.
* **Memorising the English fixes.** Real reviewers can spot scripted delivery. Use the fixes to learn structure and phrasing.
* **Only practising the questions you like.** Sticking to your strengths hides the real weaknesses.
* **Skipping the timer.** The time limit is part of what's being tested, so keep to it in practice too.
* **Changing too much at once.** Fix one thing per round so you can see progress.
* **Using stories that aren't real.** Everything must come from your own real experience. Invented stories fall apart under follow-up and won't survive a referee's verification.
