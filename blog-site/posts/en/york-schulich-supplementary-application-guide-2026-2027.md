> **Short answer:** York Schulich's supplementary application has three parts: a leadership profile (3–5 selected activities), a 5-minute timed essay and four Kira video questions with 45 seconds to prepare and 90 to answer. Schulich particularly values critical thinking and initiative without a title; give about 35% of each answer to results and reflection.

**Key points**

- Flow: OUAC (101/105) → Schulich portal → leadership profile → 5-minute essay → Kira video.
- 5-minute essay: 45 seconds to set your opening and takeaway, 210 to write, 45 to proofread.
- Kira: four questions, 45 seconds to prepare, 90 to answer.
- 90-second split: situation 10% / task 15% / action 40% / result and reflection 35%.
- Bonus: show business ethics and ESG awareness in your leadership stories.

### Timing and avoiding common traps

At top business schools, timing directly sets your ceiling. Most strong students who miss out aren't short on academic ability. They badly underestimate how complex and strategic the supplementary application is.

* **Early starters in October (the strategic advantage):**
  * **Strengths:** you have time for real **subterranean self-exploration** and to build an **experience bank** covering many kinds of experience. You can commit to one reliable source of guidance and take repeated refinement from a senior mentor, and you have room to practise on a professional video interview simulator, raising your score from a 1 to a 4 within seven days.
  * **Mindset:** calm and confident, in control of your pace and presenting your authentic self at your best.
* **Late starters in December or January (the high-risk trap):**
  * **Serious risk:** assuming the January or February deadline is far off, then scrambling over the holidays. Under time pressure you lower your standards, rewrite the essay late at night, or make only the bare minimum effort in the portal.
  * **Consequence:** rushed submissions full of clichés and vague generalities, which get screened out in the first round.

> **A coach's strategic insight**
> "In my years of coaching, admissions readers can often tell right away which essays were polished over months and which were drafts thrown together on the night of 24 December. Don't let your strategy slide from 'doing it right' into 'just getting it done before the deadline.'"

## 2. The Leadership Profile: Writing It and Polishing Three to Five Activities

### Depth over breadth: the T-model and quality over quantity

Admissions officers review hundreds of profiles every day and are already tired of cookie-cutter activities: generic student council membership, club volunteering, tutoring, debate team. Their real question is never "how many clubs did you join?" It's "how did these experiences shape the way you think and who you are?"

```
 T-model
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Breadth: 2–3 supporting activities showing varied        │
 │          interests and social responsibility             │
 └────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┘
                              │ Depth:
                              │ 1–2 anchor activities
                              │ long-term commitment + strong leadership
                              │ + concrete business or social impact
```

* **How to apply the T-model:**
  * **Breadth:** choose 2–3 supporting activities that show curiosity, the ability to work across fields and a range of perspectives.
  * **Depth:** pick 1–2 anchor activities. In these, show sustained commitment, a real leadership breakthrough and lasting impact on a community or organisation.
* **Go beyond the list: four core values to draw out.** Every entry should set aside the surface title and address these four things:
  1. **Mindset change:** the shift in understanding or new perspective the experience gave you.
  2. **Core values:** the principles and sense of responsibility you established along the way.
  3. **Measurable achievements:** clear data and concrete outputs.
  4. **Uniqueness:** the specific details that only you could have produced.

---

### Show, don't tell: strong verbs and numbers

Don't use self-declaring abstractions in your profile, such as "I'm a passionate leader." Show what you did through concrete situations, actions and results.

* **Before-and-after examples:**
  * *Weak (abstract claim or a list):* "I was president of the club, showed strong leadership and passion, and organised a fundraiser."
  * *Strong (concrete, with the numbers):* "When the club lost its funding source, I rebuilt our operating plan, launched a fundraising drive, provided 1,000 free meals to the community and kept the club running."
  * *Weak (vague):* "I volunteered at an animal shelter and helped look after the animals."
  * *Strong (quantified result):* "During my time at the shelter, I restructured the online adoption matching process, helping place 50 rescue animals with adoptive families."

* **High-impact action verbs to use:** avoid weak words like "helped," "participated in" and "was responsible for." Replace them with **Led**, **Initiated**, **Transformed**, **Pioneered**, **Spearheaded** and **Optimised**.

## 3. The Five-Minute Timed Essay: How to Handle It

### Core writing principles

In Schulich's five-minute timed essay, the biggest mistake is thinking that more words means a better answer. Long essays usually mean scattered content and filler.

* **Cut the fluff:** delete filler phrases such as "In my personal opinion" or "As a matter of fact." Every word should carry specific logic or information.
* **Focus on one core story:** don't try to fit your whole résumé into 300 seconds. One real, deep story with a clear purpose is far more convincing than ten brief mentions of activities.

---

### The 300-second battle plan

How do you stay calm and produce a strong essay under a five-minute countdown? Follow this three-stage plan:

```
 [0:00 – 0:45] Read the prompt quickly ➔ outline and draft your hook (45 seconds)
        │
        ▼
 [0:45 – 4:15] Write quickly ➔ go straight to the core story, using a STAR structure (210 seconds)
        │
        ▼
 [4:15 – 5:00] Check for typos ➔ cut filler, make sure the opening and ending connect and the logic holds (45 seconds)
```

1. **First 45 seconds: read the prompt and set your direction.** Work out what's really being asked (overcoming a setback, or handling team conflict?). Settle two things straight away: (1) your opening hook sentence, and (2) the final reflection (takeaway) your story will land on.
2. **Middle 210 seconds: write fast and densely.** Follow a situation–action–result sequence. Don't agonise over fancy words; prioritise logical flow and a complete story, and put your effort into your **specific actions** and the **shift in your thinking**.
3. **Last 45 seconds: trim and proofread.** Read the whole thing quickly, delete repetition and filler, fix spelling and grammar, and make sure the final sentence answers the prompt directly.

---

### The hook technique

Readers form their impression of an essay in the first five seconds. If the opening is flat, they mentally check out.

* **Openings to ban outright (red flags):**
  * *"I've always been passionate about business..."*
  * *"Since I was young, I wanted to be a leader..."*
* **Strong hook examples drawn from successful applications:**
  * *Example 1 (a crisis that shows resilience):* "When my experiment went badly wrong and flooded the basement of our house, I truly understood the real cost of exploring science and managing risk."
  * *Example 2 (concrete impact and business value):* "After 3D-printing the first custom prosthetic for a child in my community, I realised that combining engineering with business resources can change a life."
  * *Example 3 (a real motivation with emotional resonance):* "Growing up alongside a sibling with a rare disease, I developed a deep curiosity about how medical resources are allocated and about genetics."

## 4. The Kira Video Interview: Four Questions, 45 Seconds to Prepare, 90 Seconds to Answer

### How Kira assessment works and what it's scored on

* **The real process:** many applicants think Kira's scoring is done by AI. **In fact, Kira has more than 30,000 professionally trained human reviewers.** Its AI only helps detect spelling, grammar or technical problems. Each video answer is scored independently by several human reviewers calibrated to the specific school's rubric.
* **Pattern recognition and contrast:** a reviewer watches hundreds of videos in a season, which inevitably builds up pattern recognition. Suppose they've watched 40 answers and 32 open with the same flat line, "I've always been passionate about Schulich." Then the 33rd answer, which opens with a concrete scene and an striking detail, creates a huge contrast. That contrast is what makes an answer memorable and wins high marks.
* **The seven-day score boost:** video interviewing is a communication skill that can be trained quickly. With realistic, timed practice in a simulator, an applicant can **move from a score of 1 to a score of 4 in seven days.**

---

### The 1–5 scoring rubric in detail

| Score band | What reviewers judge | Typical answer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **No self-awareness and no deep reflection.** Vague, shallow examples; no clear sense of your own contribution; can't explain what the experience taught you. | "I sometimes help the team, and I learned leadership." (No detail, no story, no change in thinking.) |
| **2** | **Limited detail and drifting off-topic.** Examples lack support; little willingness to understand others' views; reflection stays on the surface and doesn't connect to lasting behaviour change. | Mentions a real activity but only describes the process, without explaining your specific responsibility or how you overcame a hurdle. |
| **3** | **Clear and adequate, but lacking deep insight (the safe line).** A clear, relevant example; reasonable adaptability; some summary of what you learned. | Explains the STAR sequence clearly and fluently, but the lesson is general ("I realised teamwork is important"). |
| **4** | **High initiative and mature thinking.** A thoughtful, specific example; strong initiative, adaptability and the ability to see others' point of view; a clear account of how the lesson shapes future behaviour. | Says plainly that when the project fell behind, you set up a communication system that fixed the immediate crisis and also changed how you think about delegation and trust. |
| **5** | **Vivid, authentic and deeply reflective (the top band).** Presents a lively, real example; shows maturity and calm under pressure; analyses what the lesson teaches and how it changed your long-term leadership style. | Shows calm, candour and honesty about a complete project failure. Reflects on how you used to over-control and then describes applying empowering leadership in a later project. |

> **A coach's strategic insight**
> "Here's the difference between a 1 and a 5: a 1 lacks self-awareness; a 5 is vivid, real and changes you over time. Admissions has heard enough perfect success stories. What they really want to see is how you think and mature when you face conflict, budget cuts or a failed project."

---

### STAR+T for a 90-second answer

In a 90-second answer, rambling is the biggest interview mistake. Use the **STAR+T** framework to split your time carefully:

```
 [S: Situation 10%] ──> [T: Task 15%] ──> [A: Action 40%] ──> [R/T: Result and deep reflection 35%]
  (~10 seconds)          (~15 seconds)     (~35 seconds)        (~30 seconds)
```

* **S (Situation), about 10% (roughly 10 seconds):** in one or two sentences, set the time, place and core context.
* **T (Task), about 15% (roughly 15 seconds):** state the specific goal or challenge you faced, or the responsibility you held.
* **A (Action), about 40% (roughly 35 seconds):** **the core.** Describe the specific steps you took and, importantly, **why** you took them, which shows your decision-making.
* **R/T (Result and takeaway), about 35% (roughly 30 seconds):** **extremely important.** Don't just state the final quantified outcome; also say **what the experience changed in how you think**, and what lasting lesson it gives you for the future.

---

### Delivery and on-camera presence

On video, your presence matters as much as your content.

1. **Eye contact:** look steadily into the camera lens, not at yourself on screen. Looking at the lens creates a direct connection with the human reviewer.
2. **Energy:** keep a full, confident warmth and avoid a flat or stiff tone.
3. **Clarity:** speak at a moderate pace (about 130–150 words a minute) and use clear linking phrases such as "First," "As a result," "Looking back."
4. **Expression:** stay natural and approachable, and show calm when under pressure.
5. **Gestures:** use natural, moderate gestures in front of your chest to support what you're saying. Avoid stiffness.
6. **Never recite a prepared script.**
   **Warning:** reviewers can spot recitation within seconds, from unnatural eye movement, a flat and stiff voice and pauses in odd places. Reviewers typically describe scripted answers as "really disappointing," because they show the applicant isn't willing to show their real self, and the score drops straight to the 1–2 range.

## 5. Demonstrating Core Competencies: Leadership, Resilience and Teamwork

Schulich isn't just looking for academically strong students. It's looking for innovators with the potential to become future business leaders. Weave these three core competencies into your essay and your Kira answers:

### 1. Leadership and business ethics / ESG awareness
Schulich values **initiative** that doesn't depend on an official title. Even if you're an ordinary member of a club, if you spot a pain point (duplicated processes, wasted resources), take responsibility and lead the group to a breakthrough, that is real leadership. Schulich also cares about business ethics and ESG (environmental, social and governance) issues, so when you describe your leadership, explain not only the economic value you created but also the positive difference the project made for a community, a society or a specific group. For example, you might have introduced a greener supply chain into the project or helped a disadvantaged group.

### 2. Resilience and adaptability
When you face failure, setbacks, budget cuts caused by inflation or a sudden change in circumstances, show calm composure and problem solving. What admissions most wants to see is how you adjust your strategy quickly when a project falls apart or a crisis hits, how you take responsibility and how you reflect deeply afterwards. They don't want to see you blame the environment or other team members.

### 3. Teamwork and resolving conflict
The business world is full of clashing views. When answering a question about team conflict, show how you act as a communicator and collaborator: listen to the concerns of different stakeholders, identify the root of the conflict, resolve the disagreement and find common ground. Show a mature attitude that respects diverse cultures and perspectives.

## 6. The Supplementary Application: Dos & Don'ts

| Category | Dos | Don'ts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Strategy and planning** | **Keep one reliable source of guidance (single source of truth):** work with one experienced senior mentor and refine everything on one consistent strategy, so the essay's voice stays coherent and genuine. | **Asking too many people with conflicting views:** collecting contradictory input from parents, friends and online forums can rewrite your essay out of recognition and strip out who you are (the **admission killer**). |
| **Stories and material** | **Build a varied experience bank:** draw out real stories covering leadership, setbacks, conflict and teamwork, so you're always prepared. | **Listing cookie-cutter activities:** simply stacking up sports, student council and tutoring with no depth or real change in your thinking. |
| **Expression and structure** | **Use STAR+T strictly:** split your 90 seconds carefully and put about 35% of your focus on **lessons and the shift in your thinking (takeaway)**. | **Overusing empty buzzwords:** repeating "passionate" or "strong leader" without any concrete scene or data to back them up. |
| **Verbs and results** | **Let actions and data speak (show, don't tell):** use strong verbs such as *Led, Initiated, Transformed* and quantify your impact clearly. | **Padding a timed essay with filler:** believing more words will earn higher marks, which leaves the logic scattered and shallow. |
| **Interview and presence** | **Read aloud and practise in a simulator before recording:** rehearse under real countdown and camera conditions, and make a real leap in quality within seven to fourteen days. | **Memorising a prepared script:** reciting answers makes your eyes wander and your tone stiff, and reviewers judge you as inauthentic. |
| **Use of AI** | **Keep your authentic, natural voice:** let your writing convey your maturity, your emotional detail and your reasoning exactly as you think them. | **Relying on AI to fix your language:** one-click AI polishing makes your writing flat, mechanical and formulaic, and wipes out the individuality and contrast that make you memorable. |

## FAQ

### What format is Schulich's Kira interview?
Four video questions, each with 45 seconds to prepare and 90 seconds to answer. Real reviewers score them 1–5.

### How long should the five-minute essay be?
Not as long as possible. Pick one deep story, set your opening and conclusion in the first 45 seconds, write for 210 seconds and spend the last 45 seconds cutting filler and fixing typos.

### How many activities should the Leadership Profile include?
Refine three to five: one or two long-term anchor activities plus two or three supporting activities. For each, make clear the shift in your thinking, your values, the measurable results and the unique details.

### What kind of leadership story does Schulich prefer?
Initiative that doesn't rely on a title, ideally with a positive effect on a community or society, for example by bringing in a greener supply chain or helping a disadvantaged group.
