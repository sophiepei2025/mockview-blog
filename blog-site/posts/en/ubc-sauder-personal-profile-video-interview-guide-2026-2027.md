> **Short answer:** UBC Sauder's application centres on the Personal Profile and a Kira video interview. The profile has a problem prompt, a global challenge prompt and up to five activities, scored on Engagement, Leadership, Substance and Voice; the interview is two random questions with 1–2 minutes to prepare and answer each, and no redos.

**Key points**

- Three profile parts: a problem or unfamiliar situation, a global challenge, up to five activities.
- Put your strongest anchor activity first; it sets the tone for the whole profile.
- Caring for siblings, training new staff and making study guides all count as leadership.
- Video: two random questions, 1–2 minutes to prepare and 1–2 to answer, no redos.
- Timing: situation 20% / task 15% / action 35% / result 15% / reflection 15%.

## 1. The UBC Sauder Application Process and a Full-Cycle Preparation Plan

At UBC Sauder (BCom), timing and strategy often decide the outcome. Many applicants leave their essays and video preparation until just before the December or January deadline and end up under pressure they can't manage. Stress pushes them to skip deep thinking and reduce a carefully crafted personal story to a thin list of activities. Moving from "doing it right" to "just getting it done" is one of the main reasons strong applicants fall short.

By contrast, applicants who start preparing in October have a real edge. Through **subterranean self-exploration**, they have time to dig into the real thinking behind their experiences, refine a distinctive voice and run intensive on-camera practice with a video interview simulator.

Throughout, keep to a **single source of truth**. Many applicants ask parents, teachers, Reddit and friends for conflicting opinions while writing, so the essay is rewritten again and again until it becomes a bland compromise. Following one trusted, professional strategy keeps your materials coherent and protects your authentic voice.

Here is the full preparation cycle for UBC Sauder:

```
[1. Activity selection]
          │
          ├─► Build your activity inventory using the T-model
          └─► Choose 1–2 core anchor activities and 2–3 supporting activities
          │
          ▼
[2. Drafting the essays]
          │
          ├─► Draw real situations from your answers to the core Personal Profile prompts
          └─► Identify the key turning point (catalyst) and the deep reflection in each experience
          │
          ▼
[3. Polishing and refining]
          │
          ├─► Remove clichés and red-flag phrasing
          ├─► Close the translation gap: use strong verbs and quantify your results
          └─► Keep a single source of truth to protect your distinctive voice
          │
          ▼
[4. Video interview simulator practice]
          │
          ├─► Use the STAR + takeaway structure
          └─► Practise improvised thinking, eye contact and pacing under time pressure
          │
          ▼
[5. Final review and submission]
          │
          ├─► Check that your Personal Profile and video answers are consistent (evidence building)
          └─► Submit through the official system
```

## 2. Breaking Down the Personal Profile and the T-Model

### Prompt breakdown

The UBC Sauder Personal Profile is designed to assess your reasoning, your values, your resilience and your social awareness through specific experiences. It has three core parts:

1. **Handling a difficult or unfamiliar situation:** explain in detail how you responded to a hard problem or an unfamiliar situation. State the specific actions you took, the results, and the core lesson and change in behaviour you took from it.
2. **A major global challenge:** describe what you think the most serious challenge facing the world today is, and explain what steps individuals and society as a whole should take to address it.
3. **Activities and achievements list:** list up to five activities or achievements. The range is broad: clubs, volunteering, work, sport, creative pursuits, community service or family responsibilities.

### The T-model framework

In the activities list, don't simply list everything you've done in a line. Admissions officers don't want a long, generic inventory. They want a deliberate, strategic presentation:

* **The horizontal bar (breadth):** shows the range and variety of your involvement. Choose two to three supporting activities that show a wide range of interests (sport, the arts, community service) and active participation across fields.
* **The vertical bar (depth):** shows deep commitment and real impact. Highlight one or two **anchor activities** where you invested over the long term and took real ownership and leadership. **Always list your strongest, most impactful anchor activity first**, because it sets the tone for the entire Personal Profile.

### The four assessment criteria

UBC's trained readers assess applicants mainly on four criteria:

#### Engagement
* **What's assessed:** how consistently you invested in your activities and how much personal ownership you took. Admissions doesn't reward a name on a list. It looks for real emotional and practical investment over time.

#### Leadership
* **What's assessed:** what responsibility you actually carried in an activity, and what you achieved with it.
* **Non-traditional leadership:** UBC explicitly states that leadership doesn't depend on an official title such as president, captain or chair. These non-traditional forms of leadership carry real weight:
  * training new staff proactively at a part-time job or in a daily role;
  * spotting younger students who are struggling and making and sharing study resources or tutoring materials for them;
  * taking long-term responsibility for family duties, such as caring for younger siblings or handling heavy household work.
  Any of these counts as strong leadership if it shows real initiative, solves a real problem and has a positive effect.

#### Substance and closing the translation gap
* **Specificity is the first scoring mechanism:** specificity is the main way assessors give 4 and 5 scores, because highly personal detail and concrete situations can't be faked at scale or copied from a template.
* **Closing the translation gap:** reviewers look closely at whether you move from simply **listing** facts to **translating** them into growth and a change in how you think.

> **A comparison: from listing to translating**
>
> * ❌ **Weak (listing, surface facts):**
>   *"I volunteered at an animal shelter for a year and learned the importance of teamwork and strong leadership skills."*
>   *(Why it's weak: it only says what was done, uses generic phrases, and has no detail or specific change in thinking. This is a low-scoring answer.)*
>
> * ✅ **Strong (translating, deep growth):**
>   *"Instead of just volunteering, I re-designed the shelter's adoption matching system and successfully matched 50 shelter animals with new homes, realizing that leadership is about creating structured outcomes rather than holding a title."*
>   *(Why it's strong: it gives a quantified result, 50 animals placed, shows initiative and systemic improvement, and draws out a specific insight about leadership.)*

#### Voice and the single source of truth
* **Protecting a real voice:** the writing must show you, in your own distinctive voice. Many applicants ask parents, teachers, friends and Reddit users for different opinions, so the essay loses its consistent logic in the repeated edits. Some even lose their personality and feeling through over-reliance on AI tools (voice flattening).
* **Keeping to one source of truth:** build one trusted, professional source of guidance, so your overall logic stays consistent and you don't weaken the authenticity and impact of your writing by trying to please every viewpoint.

## 3. The Video Interview: Balancing Substance and Voice

### How the interview works and what reviewers think about

UBC Sauder uses the Kira Talent video interview. The system randomly draws two improvised questions, covering leadership, team conflict, handling setbacks and personal experience. Each question gives you one to two minutes to prepare and one to two minutes to record your answer, and there are **no retakes**.

* **Human reviewers, not AI:** Kira Talent's video answers are scored by a network of more than 30,000 professionally trained human reviewers. **They are not scored by an automated AI algorithm.** (The AI on the platform only supports technical checks such as spelling and grammar. The content of the video itself is assessed entirely by human reviewers, against UBC Sauder's specific rubric.)
* **Pattern recognition and the ban on scripts:** trained human reviewers watch hundreds of videos in a single admissions season, and they develop strong **pattern recognition**. After dozens of answers, the clichés that open with "I have always been passionate about..." get flagged and ignored immediately. More importantly, **a memorised script will sink your application.** Human reviewers can tell within a second when someone is reciting: eyes drifting off the lens (staring at the screen or a note), a flat voice without natural variation, and a stiff, unnatural pace.

### The one-to-five scoring rubric

Human reviewers score video answers from 1 to 5 against a standard rubric:

* **1 (vague and superficial):** a vague or surface-level example with no awareness of your own contribution and no real reflection or learning (for example: "I sometimes help the team, and I learned leadership").
* **2 (limited detail):** a thin example that doesn't fully answer the question; little understanding of other people's perspectives; little deep reflection or connection to personal growth.
* **3 (clear but ordinary):** a clear, relevant example with reasonable adaptability and reflection, and some insight into what was learned and how you grew. It's an acceptable answer but lacks a distinctive quality that would stand out among the reviewer's pattern recognition.
* **4 (specific and thoughtful):** a considered example with real **specificity**; strong initiative, adaptability and respect for others' views; deep reflection on the lessons learned and how they shaped your future behaviour or thinking.
* **5 (vivid, real and deeply aware):** a vivid, authentic and highly reflective example; maturity, self-direction and high resilience (calm and well organised under pressure or serious setbacks); a deep account of how the experience permanently changed how you act and think.

### STAR plus reflection, with precise timing

Under time pressure, use STAR combined with a takeaway and split your time carefully. Here is the breakdown for typical 60-second and 90-second answers:

```
      [S] Situation  (20%) ── background and key facts (60s answer: ~12s | 90s answer: ~18s)
      [T] Task       (15%) ── responsibility, conflict or bottleneck (60s: ~9s  | 90s: ~13s)
      [A] Action     (35%) ── the specific actions you personally took (60s: ~21s | 90s: ~32s)
      [R] Result     (15%) ── clear outcome, team impact or numbers (60s: ~9s  | 90s: ~13s)
      [T] Takeaway   (15%) ── core insight and behaviour change (60s: ~9s  | 90s: ~14s)
```

1. **Situation (20%):** set up the background quickly (about 12 seconds in a 60-second answer; about 18 seconds in a 90-second answer).
2. **Task (15%):** state the responsibility you held, the conflict you faced or the bottleneck you had to solve (about 9 seconds in a 60-second answer; about 13 seconds in a 90-second answer).
3. **Action (35%):** describe **what specific actions you took** and **why you took them**. This is the core of the answer (about 21 seconds in a 60-second answer; about 32 seconds in a 90-second answer).
4. **Result (15%):** state the clear outcome, team impact or quantified data (about 9 seconds in a 60-second answer; about 13 seconds in a 90-second answer).
5. **Takeaway (15%):** explain the change in your thinking, saying clearly how you used to think or act and how the experience permanently changed what you do now (about 9 seconds in a 60-second answer; about 14 seconds in a 90-second answer). **These final 13 to 14 seconds of reflection decide whether your answer moves from a 3 to a 4 or 5.**

### Camera and delivery tactics

In your video interview practice, drill these camera techniques:

* **Look straight into the lens:** keep your gaze on the physical camera lens, not on the computer screen below it. Looking into the lens conveys confidence, composure and sincerity to human reviewers. Frequently glancing at the screen or looking away is picked up by reviewers' pattern recognition and read as reading from a script or lacking confidence.
* **Use deliberate one-second pauses:** between sentences, or at turning points, use a steady one-second pause in place of filler words like "um," "ah" or "like." Reviewers read a deliberate pause as calm thinking and control under pressure. Rushing through content or filling it with fillers reveals tension and anxiety.
* **Control your tone and pace:** keep natural variation in your voice and don't let nerves flatten it. Aim for about 130–150 words per minute, so that you stay clear and your delivery has quality.

## FAQ

### What parts make up the UBC Sauder Personal Profile?
One question about handling a difficult or unfamiliar situation, one question about a major global challenge, and up to five activities or achievements.

### How do I write about leadership without a formal title?
UBC explicitly recognises non-traditional leadership, such as proactively training new staff in a part-time job, making learning materials for younger students, or taking on long-term family caring responsibilities.

### How many video questions does Sauder ask?
Two random questions, covering leadership, team conflict, setbacks and personal experience. Each gives one to two minutes to prepare and one to two minutes to answer, with no retakes.

### How long should the reflection be in a 60-second answer?
About 9 seconds (about 14 seconds in a 90-second answer). Those seconds decide whether your answer stays at a 3 or reaches a 4 or 5.
