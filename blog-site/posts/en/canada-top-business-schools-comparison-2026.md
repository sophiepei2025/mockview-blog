> **Short answer:** Canada's top five undergraduate business programs all set high grade bars; the difference is what their supplementary applications reward. Queen's values self-awareness and growth, Ivey leadership and lasting impact, Sauder its four Personal Profile criteria, Rotman a clear business narrative and Schulich critical thinking. All five use Kira video interviews.

**Key points**

- Queen's Commerce admits about 6%; many 98%–99% applicants are turned down.
- Ivey AEO requires an 80%+ average in the first two years before case-method HBA in year three.
- UBC Sauder scores Engagement, Leadership, Substance and Voice across up to 5 activities.

## 1. Admissions Landscape and Overview

Applying to Canada's top undergraduate business schools has never been more competitive. With grade inflation now widespread, a 90% or even 95%+ average is no longer an advantage; it's simply a box to tick to enter the pool.

Take one of Canada's leading programs: **Queen's Commerce (Smith)** now admits only about 6%. Every year, thousands of applicants with 98%–99% averages are turned down. This shows how much the committee's logic has changed: when every candidate can handle university coursework, grades stop separating anyone, and academics no longer work as a filter.

> **The bottom line:**
> Admissions at top business schools has shifted from "can this student handle the academics?" to "how does this student think, what do they value, how self-aware are they and how do they decide under uncertainty?" That's why **supplementary applications** and **Kira Talent video interviews** are now where offers are actually won.

### 2.2 What each school looks for

### Queen's Commerce (Smith)
* **What they value:** admissions officers put great weight on **self-awareness** and genuine growth rather than inflated titles. Admitted students aren't all "student council presidents" or "captains"; a dance teacher or a lacrosse player can get in too. Queen's is explicit that elite titles don't impress; even an ordinary role is competitive if your writing shows how the experience changed your thinking and decisions and traces real growth.
* **The program:** students start on real business from day one, spend two years building a broad business foundation, and can go on exchange to partner schools around the world in third year.

### Western Ivey AEO
* **What they value:** leadership and sustained follow-through. You need to show experiences that broke the mould and created impact in high school, then keep at least an 80% cumulative average and stay involved in activities through your first two university years to move into the HBA program in third year.
* **The program:** the AEO (Advanced Entry Opportunity) model lets you explore any field (engineering, humanities, science) or a dual degree in your first two years, then move fully into the case method in third year.

### UBC Sauder
* **What they value:** the **Personal Profile** is the core of the decision. It's assessed on **Engagement, Leadership, Substance and Voice**. With at most five activities, you need to close the **translation gap**: don't just list what happened; explain how those experiences reshaped how you think.
* **The program:** broad business education resources, including the CUS Lounge (a collaborative student space) and the Canaccord Learning Commons, with a strong focus on inclusion and a global outlook.

### UofT Rotman Commerce
* **What they value:** applicants with a clear thread of business exploration and rigorous thinking. Essays and interviews should show real curiosity about business and strategic thinking, not a patchwork of unrelated activities.
* **The program:** builds on UofT's academic strength, pairing rigorous economics and business theory with hands-on practice in downtown Toronto.

### York Schulich
* **What they value:** Schulich puts **very strong emphasis on critical thinking** and a high academic baseline. Essays and interviews look at how you analyse complex business and social issues from several angles, how rigorous your logic is and your sense of social responsibility.
* **The program:** curriculum closely tied to industry needs, with a focus on applying practical business skills quickly and adapting to diverse business environments.

## 3. A Unified Process and Building Your Application Spine

### 3.1 One timeline for all five

A clear timeline keeps you out of last-minute panic and raises the quality of your essays and interviews. Don't pull all-nighters before the January or February deadlines; start early:

* **October: launch and subterranean self-exploration**
  * Go deep on your **intrinsic motivation**, and separate it from external expectations (for example, following a path just because your parents are software engineers or lawyers).
  * Use Simon Sinek's "Start with Why" to lock in your application spine.
  * Collect each school's official rubric and choose 1–2 core T-model projects (passion projects).
* **November–December: drafting and deep reflection**
  * Draft your essays. Don't list activities; focus on the breakthrough moment and how your thinking changed.
  * Cut the fluff: remove industry buzzwords and vague language.
  * Keep a **single source of truth**. Don't take conflicting advice from Reddit, relatives and friends; protect a consistent narrative and your authentic voice.
* **December–January: video interview practice**
  * Practise unscripted, timed answers in a video interview simulator.
  * Adjust your eye contact, energy, structure and pacing against the official 1–5 rubric.
  * Remove every trace of scripting and build real presence on camera.
* **January–February: final polish and submission**
  * Apply the "five polishing standards" (read aloud for flow, quantify results, align with each school's values, and so on).
  * Don't submit in the last few hours before the deadline when the system is congested; make sure everything is complete and at its best.

---

### 3.2 Your application spine and the T-model for activities

Don't present a pile of unrelated activities. Admissions isn't impressed by busyness; it's impressed by focus and impact. Build a structured **T-model**:

* **Breadth: supporting activities**
  * 2–3 secondary activities such as clubs, sports or basic volunteering.
  * Show varied interests, teamwork and energy, rounding out your profile.
* **Depth: anchor commitments and real impact**
  * Focus on 1–2 core projects.
  * **Ownership:** not just joining an existing organisation, but starting something or breaking through a key bottleneck.
  * **Solving real problems:** targeted solutions to real community or industry needs.
  * **Measurable achievements:** concrete numbers (funds raised, people served, efficiency gained).

#### Closing the translation gap: from listing to strategic translation
Most rejected applicants stay at **descriptive listing**. Successful applicants manage **strategic translation**:

* **Descriptive listing (weak):**
  * "I was president of the school business club, organised many events and developed good leadership and communication skills." (Abstract and generic, with no evidence of changed thinking.)
* **Strategic translation (strong):**
  * "When our business competition faced cancellation after a sponsor suddenly pulled out, I redesigned our sponsorship packages and pitched eight local businesses, raising $2,000. It taught me that leadership isn't giving orders; it's building a safety net of resources so the team has room to solve problems." (Shows a breakthrough moment, specific action, a quantified result and real self-reflection.)

## 4. Supplementary Essays and the Kira Talent Video Interview

### 4.1 Core rules for your essays

#### 1. Write an engaging opening hook (no clichés)
After hundreds of essays, a stale opening fatigues admissions officers instantly.
* **Clichés to avoid:**
  * ❌ *"Since I was young..."*
  * ❌ *"I've always been passionate about business..."*
  * ❌ *"I love solving problems..."*
* **What to do instead:** open with a specific defining moment, dramatic conflict or vivid detail. For example: a lab mistake that flooded the basement, or the moment you realised, after 3D-printing a prosthetic for a child in your community, that engineering and business together can change lives.

#### 2. Cut the fluff
* Don't pile on industry buzzwords (*"strong leadership skills," "highly passionate," "communication expert"*).
* **Show, don't tell:** never just claim "I'm resilient"; show it through the specific actions you took when you faced a real failure.

#### 3. Levels of reflection
The quality of your essay depends on the depth of your reflection. Aim for the highest level:

* **Level 1: descriptive**
  * Just the facts: when, where and what happened ("I organised an event and lots of people came").
* **Level 2: surface learning**
  * A stock conclusion ("This taught me teamwork is very important").
* **Level 3: analysis and vulnerability**
  * Willing to show self-doubt, a bad decision or a vulnerable moment, analysing the root cause and how it challenged your self-image.
* **Level 4–5: mindset and behavioural transformation**
  * Explains how the experience reshaped your values and thinking, and how that continues to change your decisions and future behaviour (the key to the top rubric score).

---

### 4.2 The Kira Talent video interview and STAR(T)

#### 1. Reviewer psychology and the 2024 scoring update
* **Human reviewers and pattern fatigue:** Kira Talent isn't scored by AI. **More than 30,000 trained human reviewers** score against each school's own rubric. When a reviewer watches 40 answers and 32 open with clichés, the 8 with a distinctive opening and a real story create strong **contrast**.
* **The 2024 update (total sums scoring):** in 2024 Kira introduced **total sums scoring**. A serious failure on one competency (no reflection, going off-topic) drags the final total down heavily. You can't rely on one brilliant answer to offset the others; every question has to be solid.
* **Why scripting is so damaging:** reviewers spot memorised answers within seconds: the voice flattens, the eyes drift oddly and the pauses feel stiff. Reviewers describe such answers as **disappointing and inauthentic**, and scores fall sharply.

#### 2. Delivery matters
Reviewers pay close attention to delivery as well as content:
* **Eye contact:** look steadily at the camera lens, not the screen or notes to the side.
* **Energy:** be positive, confident and enthusiastic.
* **Clarity:** speak clearly and control your pace; don't rush from nerves.
* **Expression and gestures:** natural expressions and moderate gestures; avoid stiffness.

#### 3. Timing STAR(T) for a 90-second answer
In a 1–2 minute answer, use **STAR(T)** and split your time to the second:

* **S – Situation: 10–15 seconds (about 10–15%)**
  * One or two sentences of background and context. No dragging.
* **T – Task: 10–15 seconds (about 10–15%)**
  * The core challenge, conflict or goal you faced.
* **A – Action: 35–40 seconds (about 40%, the main body)**
  * The specific actions and strategy **you personally** took, with emphasis on **why** and how you handled resistance and coordinated others.
* **R – Result: 15 seconds (about 15%)**
  * Quantified output, impact or a clearly positive outcome.
* **T – Takeaway / Reflection: 15–20 seconds (about 15–20%, the make-or-break part)**
  * **The rubric's dividing line between a 3 and a 5.** Explain how the experience changed the way you think, what limitation it revealed in you, and how it continues to shape your decisions.

## 5. An Integrated Strategy and Dos & Don'ts

### 5.1 Dos and don'ts across the whole process

| Dos | Don'ts |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Plan early (start in October):** leave time for self-exploration, rewrites, self-analysis and interview practice. | **Pull all-nighters before the deadline:** rushing in January/February badly hurts essay quality and interview performance. |
| **Keep a single source of truth:** one systematic strategy and one trusted expert to refine your work. | **Ask for too many conflicting opinions:** parents, teachers, Reddit and relatives will edit away what makes you distinctive (flattened voice). |
| **Quantify results and strengthen verbs:** use strong action verbs (initiated, transformed, engineered) and let the numbers speak. | **List every activity:** cramming every club and volunteer role into your essays makes them shallow and scattered. |
| **Practise improvising:** answer random questions on camera without notes to build natural, confident communication. | **Rely on AI polishing or memorised scripts:** your writing turns bland and impersonal, and your interview turns mechanical and loses points. |
| **Keep your authentic voice:** show the real you, your vulnerability and real growth. | **Invent stories to please admissions:** building a flawless, fake persona costs you sincerity and credibility. |

---

### 5.2 Five polishing standards

Before submitting, check your supplementary materials against these five standards:

1. **Read out loud:**
   * Read every essay aloud. If it feels clunky, slow or long-winded, a reviewer will feel the same; tighten the sentences.
2. **Elevate verbs and quantify impact:**
   * Replace weak phrasing with strong action verbs, and turn abstractions into specific numbers (for example, "organised a fundraiser" becomes "launched a community fundraiser that provided 1,000 meals for a local program").
3. **Align with each program's values:**
   * Research each school's courses, teaching model and character, so the qualities in your essays match what each program looks for (self-growth at Queen's, leadership at Ivey, Sauder's four criteria, critical thinking at Schulich).
4. **Cut the fluff:**
   * Go sentence by sentence and remove meaningless filler and empty industry phrases, so every word serves your story.
5. **Keep your narrative consistent:**
   * Check that your supplementary essays, profile, activity list and video interview present one consistent picture, building a real, coherent and irreplaceable personal story (your application spine).

## FAQ

### Do grades still matter for Canadian business programs?

Yes, but they're not enough. A 90%–95%+ average gets you into the pool; essays and the Kira interview decide offers.

### What does each school value most?

Queen's: self-awareness and growth. Ivey: leadership and ownership. Sauder: engagement, leadership, substance and voice. Rotman: business curiosity and logic. Schulich: critical thinking and social responsibility.

### What is an application spine?

One clear direction that ties your activities, essays and interview together: 1–2 deep projects plus 2–3 supporting activities in a T-shape, not a random list.

### Will I lose out without a student council title?

No. Queen's admits include dance teachers and lacrosse players; what counts is explaining how an experience changed your thinking and decisions.
